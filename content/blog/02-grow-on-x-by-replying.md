When you have a few hundred followers, almost nobody sees what you post. That is not a judgment of the writing. It is arithmetic: a post starts with your own followers, and you do not have many yet.

A reply is different. It sits under someone else's post, in front of the people that account spent years gathering. For a small account, replying is the one place where good writing meets a large audience on the same day. This is how to use it without becoming the person everyone mutes.

## Why replies carry a small account

Three things work in your favor.

**You borrow an audience.** A reply under a post that a hundred thousand people see is shown to some of those people. Your own post would be shown to a fraction of your followers.

**The author notices.** People read their replies. A handful of thoughtful ones from the same name is how strangers become familiar, and familiar people get followed back, quoted and recommended.

**The ranking has tended to reward conversation.** When Twitter published parts of its ranking code in 2023, the weights in it valued a reply well above a like, and a reply that the author answered highest of all. The system has changed since and X does not promise those numbers, but the direction is consistent with what people see: conversations travel further than applause.

## Choose where you reply

Replying everywhere is how you burn out. Choose instead.

Make a list of 20 to 30 accounts in your field. The best targets are a size or two above you, not the very largest. A post from an account with millions of followers collects a thousand replies in an hour, and yours is buried. A post from an account with twenty thousand followers gets thirty replies, and a good one is near the top.

Look for accounts where:

- the author answers replies, so a conversation is possible
- the audience is the one you want, not just a big one
- you know the subject well enough to add to it

X lets you put accounts in a list and read only that list. Do that. It turns "scrolling" into a ten-minute job.

## Reply early, but not empty

The first good replies under a post are seen by the most people, because they are there while the post is still spreading. Being early matters.

Being first with nothing matters less than it looks. "First!" and "Great post" may sit at the top for a minute, and then every reader slides past them. Aim to be among the early replies that say something. Turning on notifications for five or six accounts you care most about is enough.

## Be worth following when they click

A good reply earns a profile visit. What happens next depends on your profile, and most people lose the follow there. Check three things:

- **Your bio says what you post about**, in plain words. A reader should know in one line why following you is useful.
- **Your pinned post is your best work**, not an announcement from last year.
- **Your recent posts match your replies.** If you reply about product design and post about football, the visitor has no reason to stay.

## Follow up

Growth from replies rarely comes from one reply. It comes from the exchange after it. When the author answers you, answer back with something that moves the conversation on. When another reader replies to you, treat them as you would want to be treated. Two or three turns of a real conversation, in public, do more for you than fifty one-liners.

## Mistakes that get you muted

- **The same template under every post.** People recognize it by the second time.
- **Pitching.** A link to your product under someone else's post is an ad they did not sell you.
- **Flattery.** It is pleasant and it is invisible.
- **Arguing to win.** Disagree, give your reason and let it rest.
- **Volume over thought.** Fifty forgettable replies are worth less than five good ones, and they teach people to skip your name.

## Measure the right thing

Likes on a reply are a weak signal. The numbers that matter are profile visits and new followers. X's post analytics show how many people who saw a reply went on to open your profile. If a reply was seen by many and visited by none, it was pleasant but gave nobody a reason to look closer.
