Advice about replying on X often comes as a number: reply 50 times a day, reply 100 times a day. The number is the least useful part. Fifty replies that nobody reads do less than five that start conversations, and they take ten times as long.

What works is a routine you can keep up, at a volume where every reply is still worth reading. This article gives realistic numbers for three kinds of user and a 20-minute routine you can start tomorrow.

## There is no magic number

Nobody outside X knows a threshold at which replies "start working", and anyone who quotes one is guessing. What can be said honestly:

- A reply only helps if someone reads it and thinks better of you.
- The number of good replies you can write is limited by what you know and how much time you have.
- A habit kept for months beats a burst kept for a week.

So the right number is the largest one at which you would still be happy to see all of that day's replies side by side on your profile.

## Three realistic levels

### Starting out: about 5 a day

Five considered replies a day is a real habit. It takes fifteen to twenty minutes, and over a month it puts your name under 150 conversations in your field. At this level you learn what gets answered, which is worth more than volume.

Five is also where a free tool is enough. [XAICG](/) gives {{free}} comments a day at no cost, so a beginner's routine fits inside the free plan.

### A serious daily habit: 20 to 50 a day

This is where people who use X for work tend to settle: founders, creators, people in sales or recruiting. Spread across two or three short sessions, it is roughly an hour a day of writing by hand, and much less with good drafts to start from.

At this level variety starts to matter. Use different [types of reply](/blog/reply-types-that-start-conversations), and do not let any two look alike.

### Teams and several accounts: 100 or more a day

A person running a brand account, a founder's account and their own, or an agency working for clients, can reach a hundred replies a day without any single account doing anything unusual. The volume is spread across accounts and people.

What to watch here is sameness. A hundred replies from one account in a day, all in one shape, is the pattern that makes readers and platforms suspicious. See [is it safe to use AI for replies on X](/blog/is-it-safe-to-use-ai-replies-on-x).

## A 20-minute routine

Do this once a day. Twice if you are at the second level.

**Minutes 0 to 3: your own notifications.** Answer the people who replied to you first. They have already shown interest, and an exchange with them is worth more than a new reply to a stranger.

**Minutes 3 to 6: open your list.** Keep a list of 20 to 30 accounts in your field and read only that. If you do not have one yet, [growing on X by replying](/blog/grow-on-x-by-replying) explains how to choose them.

**Minutes 6 to 16: write the replies.** Pick the posts where you have experience, a fact or a real question. Skip the rest without guilt. Two minutes each is plenty.

**Minutes 16 to 20: follow up.** Go back to yesterday's replies. If an author answered, answer them. This is the step most people skip and the one that produces followers.

## Batch, do not graze

Checking X forty times a day to reply once each time costs far more attention than the replies are worth. Two or three fixed sessions do the same work and leave the rest of your day alone. Morning and late afternoon cover most time zones well enough.

Notifications for a handful of accounts you most want to reach are the one exception, because early replies are seen by more people.

## When to stop for the day

Stop when any of these is true:

- You are replying to posts you have nothing to say about.
- Your last three replies have the same shape.
- You are skimming posts instead of reading them.

Past that point each extra reply lowers the average, and the average is what people judge you by.

## Start smaller than you think

Begin with five a day for two weeks. If you kept it up and the replies were good, raise it. If you missed days, the number was too high, and a smaller habit you keep will do more for you than a bigger one you drop.
