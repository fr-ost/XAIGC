How to Write Better Replies on X (Twitter)

A practical guide to writing replies on X that people read and answer. React to one detail, add something new, keep it short and skip the filler.

November 14, 2026 · 4 min read · Writing

Most replies on X are never read past the first three words. That is not because people are unkind. It is because most replies say nothing the post did not already say. "Great point!" and "So true" are everywhere, and readers learn to slide over them.

A reply is a very small piece of writing with one job: give the author, or the people reading along, a reason to answer. This guide is about doing that job well.

Start from one detail in the post

The fastest way to stand out is to prove you read the post. Pick one concrete thing in it, a number, a claim, a word choice, a missing step, and respond to that.

Say the post is:

We moved our weekly planning from Monday morning to Friday afternoon. Mondays are now for doing, not deciding.

A weak reply could sit under any post about work:

Love this. Planning is so important for teams!

A stronger reply could only sit under this one:

Did Friday planning survive the first week where something broke on a Thursday? That is where ours fell apart.

The second reply names the detail (Friday), tests it against real life and gives the author an easy question. It took one more sentence of thought, not more words.

Add something the post did not have

Before you write, ask what you can bring. Usually it is one of five things:

  • Experience. You tried the thing. Say what happened.
  • Information. A number, a source or an example the author left out.
  • A question. Something specific that the author can answer in a line.
  • A different view. Where you think the post is wrong or incomplete, and why.
  • A laugh. A joke that is about the post, not a stock one.

If you have none of the five, a like is the honest response. Not every post needs your reply, and people who reply only when they have something to say are the ones whose replies get read.

Keep it short enough to read at a glance

Replies are read in a column, between other replies. One or two sentences is the natural size. Three is a lot. If your point needs a paragraph, it is probably a post of your own, and you can say so: "This deserves a longer answer, writing it up."

Cut the warm-up. "I think that", "In my opinion", "Just my two cents" and "As someone who" can nearly always go. Start at the point.

Write the way you talk

Read the reply out loud. If you would not say it to the person across a table, rewrite it. Spoken language is short, uses contractions and leaves things out. That is what sounds human on X.

Formal words are the usual giveaway. People say "use", not "utilize". They say "big", not "significant". They say "I was wrong about this", not "this challenged my assumptions".

Ask questions that are easy to answer

A good question is narrow. "What do you think about remote work?" asks the author to write an essay. "Did you keep the Friday meeting when the team went remote?" asks for one word and invites a second sentence.

Avoid the engagement question tacked on the end. "What do you all think?" under your own reply reads as a request for attention, and people notice.

Disagree without starting a fight

Disagreement is one of the most valuable things a reply can offer, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Three habits help:

  1. Disagree with the claim, not the person. "This breaks down above 50 people" is about the idea.
  2. Give your reason in the same breath. A bare "wrong" is a heckle.
  3. Leave a door open. "Unless you have seen it work at that size?" turns a correction into a conversation.

What to leave out

  • Generic praise: "Great post", "This", "So true", "Well said"
  • A summary of the post, handed back to its author
  • Hashtags. They do nothing in a reply.
  • A link to your own product, unless someone asked
  • A row of emojis in place of a thought
  • Your credentials, unless they are the reason your point is worth hearing

A ten-second check before you press Reply

  • Could this reply sit under a different post? If yes, make it more specific.
  • Does it add experience, information, a question, a different view or a laugh?
  • Would I say it out loud?
  • Is the first word doing any work?
